\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{Chapter2}

\section{Review of Existing Methods}
Electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a powerful neurophysiological technique for the
objective evaluation of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) due to its high temporal resolution,
non-invasive nature, and sensitivity to brain functional changes. Over the past decade, numerous
machine learning and deep learning approaches have been proposed for EEG-based depression
detection. These methods can be broadly categorized into three groups: convolutional neural
network (CNN)-based models, attention and transformer-based architectures, and graph-based learning
frameworks. This section reviews representative works from each category, highlights their
limitations, and motivates the proposed graph-enhanced transformer with soft connectivity bias.

\subsection{CNN-Based EEG Depression Detection}
Early applications of deep learning in medical diagnostics were predominantly based on CNNs, which
were employed for the automatic extraction of spatial and spectral features from multi-channel EEG
signals. Acharya \emph{et al.}~\cite{acharya2018} demonstrated that CNNs trained on minimally
processed EEG data could outperform traditional classifiers relying on handcrafted features for
depression screening.

Jiang and colleagues~\cite{jiang2021eeg} put forward a spatial feature enhancement framework that
represents inter-electrode spatial relationships and thereby enhances the classification of
depression. Through the merging of spatial mapping with machine learning classifiers, their
research proved that the use of electrode topology results in more distinguishing representations
than using independently treated EEG channels. This exposes the drawback of earlier CNN-based
models which depend purely on grid-like channel arrangements without explicit spatial awareness.

Following this direction, Seal \emph{et al.} introduced DeprNet~\cite{seal2021}, a CNN-based
framework that improved robustness by directly learning representations from noisy raw EEG signals.
Subsequently, CNN-based models were extended to multi-disorder diagnosis tasks, including
depression and schizophrenia, confirming their applicability across psychiatric
conditions~\cite{wang2022}.

Despite their effectiveness, CNN-based approaches are inherently constrained by local receptive
fields and often treat EEG channels as grid-like structures. This design limits their ability to
capture long-range inter-channel dependencies that are critical for understanding large-scale
brain dynamics. To mitigate this limitation, spatio-temporal fusion strategies have been proposed,
combining CNNs with temporal modeling components to jointly represent spatial and sequential EEG
characteristics~\cite{zhang2023}.

Specialized CNN architectures have also been used to extract robust features from noisy and
nonlinear EEG signals. He \emph{et al.}~\cite{he2023hmt} introduced the HMT model, a CNN-based
framework that is capable of dealing with non-stationary and noise-corrupted EEG data. Xue
\emph{et al.}~\cite{xue2022feature} also used deep learning along with feature extraction techniques
to improve EEG classification performance. These methods, while able to provide robustness and
accuracy, at the same time focus primarily on channel-level representations and do not take into
account the inter-regional interactions that are known to be disrupted in depressive disorders.

The use of hybrid deep learning architectures has been studied to characterize spatial and temporal
EEG features together. Qayyum \emph{et al.}~\cite{qayyum2020hybrid} put forward a combined
deep--shallow network that merges convolutional layers with gated recurrent units (GRUs) to analyze
EEG signals for both spatial patterns and temporal dynamics. Their findings show that the
application of recurrent parts alongside CNNs improves the performance of depression assessment,
especially when time-related factors have diagnostic importance. Still, hybrid models lack the
means to clearly display brain connectivity, which makes them less interpretable in terms of
neurophysiology.

However, these hybrid models still lack an explicit mechanism to characterize brain connectivity
networks, which are known to be altered in depression, thereby restricting their ability to fully
exploit functional interactions.

\subsection{Attention and Transformer-Based Architectures}
Attention mechanisms were introduced to overcome the locality limitations of convolutional models
by enabling networks to focus on informative EEG regions. Yang \emph{et al.}~\cite{yang2023}
proposed a gated temporal-separable attention network that enhanced depression recognition by
emphasizing discriminative EEG segments. More recently, transformer architectures have gained
prominence due to their self-attention mechanism, which is capable of modeling long-range temporal
dependencies. Tigga and Garg~\cite{tigga2022} showed that transformer-based attention models
outperform recurrent architectures in EEG-based depression detection by effectively capturing
global temporal relationships.

Alongside attention-based learning, interpretability has surfaced as a primary need for clinical
acceptance of deep learning models. Yin \emph{et al.}~\cite{yin2023brainage} developed a deep
learning framework that is anatomically interpretable, connecting learned representations with
recognized brain regions. Their research was primarily centered on brain age estimation, yet it
proves the importance of anatomical constraints for increasing model interpretability and
maintaining clinical significance. These methods highlight the necessity of explainable
representations in the application of deep learning to neuropsychiatric disorders, depression
being one of them.

Research efforts have also focused on developing lightweight and multi-scale transformer
architectures to balance performance and computational efficiency. Hou \emph{et al.}~\cite{hou2025}
proposed a convolutional transformer that achieved competitive results while maintaining low
complexity, making it suitable for practical applications. Similarly, Ying \emph{et al.}~\cite{ying2024}
introduced an EEG-driven attention model that improved feature learning and generalization across
subjects. Although transformer-based approaches effectively model temporal dependencies, most
existing methods process EEG channels independently or concatenate features across channels,
without explicitly incorporating inter-regional functional connectivity.

Recurrent neural networks have also been studied for explainability. Zhang \emph{et al.}~\cite{zhang2024rnn}
proposed an explainable and generalizable RNN framework that differentiates human brain states from
EEG signals. Their approach marks the important time sequences based on which classification took
place, making the decision process easier to interpret while maintaining performance. However,
recurrent and attention-based models do not provide explicit representations of functional
connectivity between EEG channels.

\subsection{Graph-Based Learning for EEG Depression Detection}
Graph neural networks (GNNs) provide a natural framework for modeling EEG signals by representing
electrodes as nodes and functional or spatial relationships as edges. Chen \emph{et al.}~\cite{chen2022}
proposed a self-attention graph pooling approach that leveraged EEG topological structures during
learning, leading to improved depression detection performance. Other studies have demonstrated
that graph convolutional networks can effectively exploit spatial information and inter-channel
relationships, outperforming conventional CNN- and RNN-based models~\cite{wang2024gctnet}.

Graph-based learning with explainability has gained traction for EEG-based depression recognition.
Shen \emph{et al.}~\cite{shen2023explainable} proposed an explainable graph convolutional network
that represents EEG channels as graph nodes and connects them through functional relationships.
The proposed method not only yielded classification performance comparable to the best methods but
also highlighted the brain areas and connections that played a role in recognizing depression.
This result confirms that graph-based models are appropriate for illustrating the network-level EEG
disorder in MDD.

Recent research has further explored adaptive and temporal graph learning techniques to capture
dynamic brain connectivity patterns. Luo \emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2023} suggested that adaptive graph
structures learned during training can enhance recognition of subject-specific connectivity
patterns. Time--frequency-aware graph learning methods, such as TFAGL~\cite{xu2025}, incorporate
spectral dynamics into graph representations, improving robustness to EEG noise and inter-subject
variability. Graph autoencoders have also been employed to embed functional brain networks into
compact latent representations, offering improved interpretability for MDD diagnosis~\cite{noman2024}.
Despite these advances, many graph-based approaches rely on hard graph constructions or explicit
graph convolution operations, which impose strict topological constraints. Such methods are highly
sensitive to noise in functional connectivity estimation and are prone to overfitting, particularly
when training data are limited or connectivity measures are unreliable due to short temporal
windows.

Deep learning frameworks have been used to analyze several neuropsychiatric disorders at the same
time. Zhang~\cite{zhang2024iccece} studied identification of schizophrenia and depression together
through EEG-based deep learning models and tested cross-disorder representation. On the same line,
Hisana Thasneem \emph{et al.}~\cite{thasneem2023classification} compared the performance of deep
learning frameworks for EEG signal classification of neurological disorders. These investigations
show that deep learning power for EEG analysis is diverse, but disorder-specific modeling strategies
that depict distinctive connectivity patterns of depression should be used.

\section{Comparative Analysis of Existing Studies}
A comparative study of existing EEG-based depression detection methods shows a wide variety of
models, including convolutional and recurrent neural networks, as well as graph structures and
transformers. The main differences between the various methods are in the EEG signal
representation, the ability to detect spatial and temporal dependencies, and the incorporation of
neuroscientific theories. Typically, CNNs are used for spatial feature extraction, while recurrent
models and attention-based techniques are used for temporal dynamics across EEG segments. Recently,
graph-based learning has been used to capture inter-channel relationships explicitly through
functional connectivity measures.

Many existing methods are often using static connectivity graphs as a basis or applying attention
mechanisms that are purely data-driven. Thus, their ability to grasp and adapt to changes in the
brain network associated with depression suffers greatly. Factors like data selection, validation
strategies per subject, and performance metrics differ across studies and make direct comparisons
difficult. To overcome this challenge, a synthesis matrix is provided as a tool to compare previous
studies by feature representation, model architecture, evaluation protocol, and reported
limitations, revealing the most important trends and gaps in research. A comprehensive analysis of

\section{Motivation for Graph-Enhanced Transformers with Soft Connectivity Bias}
Recently, hybrid models combining graph learning and transformer architectures have shown promising
results in EEG-based depression detection. STGE-Former~\cite{stgeformer2025} represents a notable
example that integrates spatial--temporal graph representations with transformer-based modeling.
However, STGE-Former and similar approaches still rely on explicit graph convolution operations,
making their performance heavily dependent on the accuracy of the constructed graphs. While hard
graph enforcement introduces strong structural constraints, incorporating graph information as a
soft bias within the transformer attention mechanism provides a more flexible alternative.

\section{Conclusion}
By treating functional connectivity as prior information rather than a fixed structure, the
attention mechanism can adaptively emphasize, down-weight, or ignore connections based on their
relevance. This design is particularly well suited for EEG-based depression detection, where
functional connectivity estimates can vary significantly across time windows and subjects.
Motivated by these observations, this work proposes a graph-enhanced transformer framework that
integrates Pearson correlation-based functional connectivity as a soft attention bias. This
approach aims to balance topological awareness with model flexibility, addressing key limitations
of existing CNN-, transformer-, and GNN-based methods while improving generalization in subject-wise
evaluation settings.
